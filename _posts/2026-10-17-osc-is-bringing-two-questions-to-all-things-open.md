Next week Open Source Collective will be at All Things Open in Raleigh. Caitlin and I will be at our table (#2) near the Attendee Lounge on Monday, October 19, and Tuesday, October 20. As with last year, we’re not giving a talk; instead, we are coming to have conversations. 

We have one question for maintainers and funders: what does it take for the software we all depend on to last?

**For maintainers: what would $1,000 do?**

Open Source Collective is a nonprofit fiscal host that provides the legal and financial infrastructure for 2,500+ open source projects, so maintainers can accept funds, pay people and vendors, sign contracts, and stay focused on the work. Every day we see something that rarely makes it into conference talks. Money matters, but what does sustainability look like?

So at our table we are asking maintainers a simple question. If your project received $1,000, what would you do with it? Pay a contributor for a week of issue triage? Cover hosting costs? Put it toward a documentation sprint, or help a new maintainer travel to their first conference? There is no wrong answer. We are giving $1,000 to one project as part of our time at ATO, and we want to hear how maintainers think about putting money to work.

**For funders: how do you decide what to fund?**

The second question is for the companies, foundations, and public bodies that want to support open source and aren’t sure where to start. You depend on thousands of packages, so which ones matter most? Do you fund the projects everyone recognizes, or the quieter ones underneath them? How much, how long, and through what process, so that procurement and legal can say yes?

Funding works best when it follows real use: the dependencies your organization actually runs on, including ones that don't show up in a keynote. You can measure that now. Ecosyste.ms publishes open data and APIs that map software interdependencies, offering insights into usage, creation, and impact. It pulls together data from package registries, software repositories, vulnerability databases, containers, and operating systems, so you can see which projects are part of the critical software ecosystems that form the backbone of our digital infrastructure, and which of those keep your business running.

Funding also works best when it recurs. Infrastructure is not a one-time purchase. If your business would feel it when a project goes quiet, that project is already part of your continuity plan, whether or not it is in your budget.

At ATO we are offering one funder a funding intelligence session. We'll start with a conversation about your organization, what you depend on, and what kind of support makes sense for you. Then we’ll walk you through how ecosyste.ms can map your dependencies and give you a report to take to your leadership. It's the conversation we wish more funders had before they write the first check.

**Why both sides need the same home**

Maintainers and funders are working on the same problem from opposite ends.

Maintainers who get funding have to figure out how to receive it, pay the contributor who’s been triaging issues, and respond when a sponsor’s procurement team sends a vendor form. A funder who wants to support that maintainer needs someone who can sign the contract, take the payment, and show where the money went. 

That’s the job of a fiscal host. Projects we host can take in money, pay contributors and vendors, and sign agreements without setting up their own legal entity. Every budget on Open Collective is public, so maintainers, funders, and anyone else can see what came in and where it went. Fiscal hosting is the middleware that lets money move cleanly in both directions. People are more important than code, and the people who maintain critical software deserve the same practical infrastructure the rest of the working world takes for granted. Accounting. Contracts. Employment pathways. Someone to call when the paperwork gets strange.

None of this gets solved at a conference, but it’s a good place to have these conversations. ATO brings together maintainers, community leaders, and the companies that build on open source. We want to hear what is working for you, what is getting in your way, and where a fiscal host could make your work easier. We’re there to listen.
